# Exoplanets: Worlds Beyond the Solar System

An **exoplanet** is a planet that orbits a star other than the Sun. We usually
cannot take a resolved photograph of the planet: the star is far brighter, and
the system is far away. Instead, exoplanet science often starts by measuring a
small, repeatable effect of the planet on light or motion from the star.

## What can we observe?

The planet changes what an observer receives from the star. Different methods
turn different changes into evidence:

| Method | What changes | What the change can constrain |
|---|---|---|
| Transit | received starlight gets slightly dimmer | planet-to-star radius ratio and orbital timing |
| Radial velocity | stellar spectral lines shift as the star moves | a mass-related orbital signal |
| Direct imaging | light from the planet is separated from stellar light | brightness and atmosphere in favorable systems |

COMP 521 begins with transits because they connect a visible measurement to a
geometric model that we can calculate, test, and gradually improve.

## The key scientific habit

The observation is not “we saw a planet.” The observation is a pattern in the
data—for example, a repeatable decrease in received starlight. A model links
that pattern to a possible physical explanation. Good scientific computing
keeps those two pieces distinct:

- **observable:** the received light as a function of time;
- **model:** a prediction for how an orbiting planet blocks some of the star;
- **inference:** what properties are consistent with the observation and the
  model;
- **limits:** other phenomena or model choices can sometimes make similar
  signals.
